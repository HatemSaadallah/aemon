## 1. arri-base (present day, from the test step)

**Question:** Should ARRI develop a differentiated proposition of its own for individual creators, beyond its professional cinema range and its smartphone partnership, or keep its consumer presence to partners? What evidence would justify either choice?

- **T1:** Individual creators will adopt ARRI products if they perceive them as a status symbol and a differentiator for their work, leading to higher purchase intent and willingness to pay.
- **T2:** Individual creators will adopt ARRI products if they perceive them as a necessary tool for their craft, leading to higher purchase intent and willingness to pay when priced competitively.

**Sources read (dossier date):** arri-alexa-price (2025-08-07), arri-cined-challenges (2025-12-01), arri-core-focus (2025-12-16), arri-honor-milestone (2026-08-12), arri-ymc-use-not-own (2026-09-29)

**Test:** ARRI Creator Perception Test. Online survey with between‑subjects experimental design. Participants are individual creators (YouTubers, indie filmmakers, content creators) recruited via creator platforms. Randomly assigned to either Status Frame (ARRI presented as prestige brand, emphasizing exclusivity, professional status, premium price) or Necessity Frame (ARRI presented as essential tool for professional‑grade results, emphasizing technical specs, reliability, competitive pricing). Each participant sees a product description, price point (€4,999 for a simplified camera model), and a purchase scenario. Measures: (1) Willingness to Pay (WTP) – maximum price they would pay; (2) Purchase Intent – 7‑point Likert (very unlikely to very likely); (3) Recommendation likelihood – 7‑point Likert.

**Sample:** 300 creators, 150 per frame, recruited via creator community panels (estimated cost €12,000 for panel access and incentives).  
**Measure:** Primary outcome: difference in mean WTP between Status and Necessity frames. Secondary: difference in Purchase Intent score.  
**Cost:** €20000, 12 weeks; theories tested: T1, T2

- **Predicts T1:** Mean WTP in Status frame > Mean WTP in Necessity frame by at least €500 (e.g., €5,500 vs €5,000). Purchase Intent score in Status frame > Necessity frame by at least 1 point.
- **Predicts T2:** Mean WTP in Necessity frame >= Mean WTP in Status frame (difference <= €200). Purchase Intent score in Necessity frame > Status frame by at least 1 point.

**Decision rule:** If observed WTP difference favors Status frame by ≥ €500, adopt T1 and recommend a differentiated premium creator proposition. If observed WTP difference favors Necessity frame by ≥ €500, adopt T2 and recommend a competitively priced creator offering. If difference is within ±€200, the test is inconclusive; proceed to a follow‑up qualitative exploration of creator motivations.

**Teacher, learned:** We have read three sources. ARRI’s professional cinema cameras are gold standard but traditionally rented, not owned by individual creators (arri-ymc-use-not-own). ARRI’s core focus remains high‑end cinema (arri-core-focus). It has a smartphone partnership with HONOR, showing some consumer reach (arri-honor-milestone). No direct evidence of individual creator demand, willingness to pay, or status perception exists in the dossier.

**Teacher, uncertain:** Two theories compete: T1 argues creators buy ARRI for status and premium pricing; T2 argues they buy for functional necessity and competitive pricing. The evidence so far only shows current market position, not motivation. A test must reveal which driver dominates.

**Teacher, why next:** With no searches left, the next step is to run a low‑cost experiment that isolates status vs necessity framing and measures willingness to pay and purchase intent. The €20k budget and 12‑week timeline allow a targeted online survey with a between‑subjects design. The result will either support T1 (higher WTP/purchase intent in status frame) or T2 (higher WTP/purchase intent in necessity frame), providing the evidence needed to decide on a differentiated creator proposition.

**Flags:** stated preference only; the arms differ in framing or message

## 2. superior-new-evidence-irrelevant (present day, from the test step)

**Question:** Should Superior Glove develop a differentiated consumer proposition for a specific use case, beyond its existing work-glove offering? What evidence would justify that investment?

- **T1:** Consumers will purchase Superior Glove gloves if they perceive them as high‑quality, safety‑certified PPE, because they trust the brand’s reputation for cut‑resistant materials and work‑glove expertise.
- **T2:** Consumers will purchase Superior Glove gloves if they perceive them as a premium, lifestyle product that reflects personal style and status, because they value brand association with quality and professionalism.

**Sources read (dossier date):** superior-cut-specialist (2014-10-27), superior-distribution-only (2014-10-27), superior-nc-plant (2025-01-14), superior-wholesale (unknown (accessed 2026-11-02))

**Test:** Safety vs Lifestyle Proposition Test. Online choice experiment with refundable deposit for a prototype glove

**Sample:** 400 adult consumers recruited via panel (200 target outdoor/DIY, 200 target lifestyle/urban users). Stratify by age, gender, and region to reflect potential markets.  
**Measure:** Average willingness to pay (WTP) in euros for each version: (a) safety‑certified version (highlighting cut‑resistance, Kevlar/Dyneema, safety certifications); (b) lifestyle version (highlighting premium materials, design, brand prestige, fashion colors). Deposit: €20 refundable if they decline purchase after seeing the prototype. Track conversion to actual purchase (full price €45 for safety, €55 for lifestyle).  
**Cost:** €20000, 12 weeks; theories tested: T1, T2

- **Predicts T1:** Safety version WTP ≥ €45 and ≥ €10 higher than lifestyle version; conversion rate ≥ 15%.
- **Predicts T2:** Lifestyle version WTP ≥ €55 and ≥ €10 higher than safety version; conversion rate ≥ 15%.

**Decision rule:** If safety version WTP ≥ €45 and exceeds lifestyle version by ≥ €10 → recommend safety‑focused consumer proposition. If lifestyle version WTP ≥ €55 and exceeds safety version by ≥ €10 → recommend lifestyle proposition. If neither version meets its target or the difference is < €10 → recommend no expansion (continue B2B).

**Teacher, learned:** We have identified two competing theories about why consumers might buy Superior Glove gloves: T1 – safety‑certified PPE reputation drives purchase; T2 – premium lifestyle/status drives purchase. Evidence so far is limited to company claims about cut‑resistance, Kevlar/Dyneema licensing, and wholesale distribution. No direct consumer demand, price sensitivity, or existing retail presence has been found. With zero searches left, we must decide whether a €20k, 12‑week test can resolve the uncertainty.

**Teacher, uncertain:** Which consumer proposition (safety‑certified vs. lifestyle) would generate sufficient willingness to pay to justify investment, and does Superior Glove have a viable route to reach individual buyers?

**Teacher, why next:** A low‑cost experiment that measures real willingness to pay for two distinct value propositions can discriminate between T1 and T2. If one proposition yields significantly higher WTP and the difference exceeds a predefined threshold, the company can prioritize that route. If neither meets the threshold, the safer choice is to stay in the B2B model. The test fits the budget and timeline and uses a real commitment (deposit) to capture consequential behavior rather than stated intent.

**Flags:** asks for a real commitment (deposit, pre-order, paid use)

## 3. hilti-new-evidence-irrelevant (present day, from the test step)

**Question:** Should Hilti develop a differentiated proposition for home users or hobbyists, beyond its existing professional offering, or leave that market alone? What evidence would justify either choice?

- **T1:** Homeowners and hobbyists will adopt Hilti tools if they perceive them as reliable and professional-grade, and are willing to pay a premium for durability.
- **T2:** Homeowners and hobbyists will adopt Hilti tools if they perceive them as reliable and professional-grade, and are willing to pay a premium for durability, but will also be sensitive to price and seek the best value.

**Sources read (dossier date):** hilti-b2b-reach (unknown (reader comments begin 2023-11-08)), hilti-consumer-market-size (unknown (written while the COVID-19 pandemic was ongoing)), hilti-consumers-price-sensitive (unknown (written while the COVID-19 pandemic was ongoing)), hilti-craftsman-pro-label (2004-11-22), hilti-direct-model (unknown (accessed 2026-11-02)), hilti-homeowners-share (2022-05-10), hilti-price-driver (2022-05-10)

**Test:** Premium vs. Low-Cost Tool Purchase Intent Test. Online controlled experiment with a landing page offering a Hilti professional-grade tool (e.g., cordless drill) at a premium price (€199) and a comparable low-cost alternative (e.g., generic brand) at a lower price (€99). Participants will be screened to be homeowners or hobbyists who have purchased power tools in the past year. The page will highlight professional-grade reliability and durability for the Hilti offer, and basic specs for the low-cost option. After viewing the offer, participants will be asked to indicate purchase intent (1‑10 scale) and whether they would commit to a pre-order deposit of €20 (refundable).

**Sample:** 500 participants, recruited via a panel matching U.S. homeowner demographics (age 25–64, income $50k‑$150k). Randomly assigned to see either Hilti premium or low-cost offer.  
**Measure:** Average purchase intent score and deposit conversion rate for each offer.  
**Cost:** €20000, 12 weeks; theories tested: T1, T2

- **Predicts T1:** Higher intent and deposit rate for Hilti premium offer than low-cost alternative.
- **Predicts T2:** Higher intent and deposit rate for Hilti premium offer than low-cost alternative, but the gap will be smaller than predicted by T1 due to price sensitivity.

**Decision rule:** If the Hilti premium offer achieves an average intent score >= 7 (on 1‑10) and deposit conversion >= 15%, proceed to a pilot launch of a consumer-focused product line (e.g., a lower-cost Hilti sub-brand). If the gap between Hilti and low-cost is < 3 points on intent or < 10% on deposit conversion, or if deposit conversion for Hilti is < 10%, decline expansion and focus on professional segment.

**Teacher, learned:** We have identified two open theories about homeowner adoption of Hilti tools: T1 posits premium willingness based on professional-grade reputation; T2 adds price sensitivity as a secondary factor. Evidence shows consumers are price-sensitive and prefer cheaper imports, challenging both theories. No evidence yet directly links Hilti’s professional reputation to consumer willingness to pay a premium. Unread sources may contain data on Hilti’s consumer presence, brand perception, or market experiments that could support or refute the theories.

**Teacher, uncertain:** The key uncertainty is whether Hilti’s professional brand can overcome consumer price sensitivity to drive premium purchases. We lack direct evidence of consumer willingness to pay for Hilti’s professional attributes, or of any existing consumer-facing initiatives.

**Teacher, why next:** A low-cost test can measure actual purchase intent for a professional-grade Hilti tool at a premium price versus a low-cost alternative, directly testing the core prediction of both T1 and T2. The test would also reveal whether price remains a barrier even when professional reputation is highlighted. This experiment fits the €20,000 budget and 12‑week timeline, and its outcome would inform whether to proceed with a differentiated consumer proposition or stay focused on the professional segment.

**Flags:** asks for a real commitment (deposit, pre-order, paid use); rule leads straight to developing or launching

## 4. technogym-new-evidence-irrelevant (present day, from the test step)

**Question:** Should Technogym develop a differentiated proposition for home users, beyond its existing premium home equipment and apps, or keep its current consumer offer? What evidence would justify either choice?

- **T1:** Home users will adopt a differentiated proposition if they perceive it as a premium lifestyle experience rather than just a functional product.
- **T2:** Home users will adopt a differentiated proposition if they experience a measurable health benefit that cannot be achieved with existing generic equipment.

**Sources read (dossier date):** technogym-500k-homes (2025-03-26), technogym-athletech-b2b (2025-03-26), technogym-channels (2025-03-26), technogym-consumer-headwind (2025-03-26), technogym-cw-price-subs (2022-12-12), technogym-ggr-search-down (unknown (covers 2022 to 2023 search data)), technogym-home-design (unknown (accessed 2026-11-03)), technogym-peloton-2021-gyms (2021-11-05)

**Test:** Lifestyle vs. Health Prototype Test. Online controlled experiment with two product mock‑ups and a refundable deposit to measure real purchase intent.

**Sample:** 200 participants recruited via fitness‑focused panels, split 100 per variant. Participants must be homeowners or renters with space for a home gym and have purchased fitness equipment in the past 2 years.  
**Measure:** Conversion to a €50 refundable deposit (i.e., % of participants who pay the deposit) and average willingness‑to‑pay (WTP) bid in an open‑ended auction after viewing the prototype. Secondary measure: self‑reported intent score (1‑10).  
**Cost:** €20000, 12 weeks; theories tested: T1, T2

- **Predicts T1:** Design‑focused prototype will achieve ≥15% higher deposit conversion and ≥15% higher average WTP than the health‑focused prototype.
- **Predicts T2:** Health‑focused prototype will achieve ≥20% higher deposit conversion and ≥20% higher average WTP than the design‑focused prototype.

**Decision rule:** If the design prototype’s conversion exceeds the health prototype’s by ≥15 percentage points, adopt a lifestyle‑centric differentiated proposition. If the health prototype’s conversion exceeds the design prototype’s by ≥20 percentage points, adopt a health‑outcome‑centric proposition. If neither threshold is met, retain the current consumer offer and gather more evidence before further investment.

**Teacher, learned:** We confirmed Technogym already reaches 500,000 private homes and its consumer segment is growing (+8% H1 2024). However, overall home‑gym equipment trends are down, and price/subscription sensitivity is evident. Two plausible drivers for a differentiated home proposition are status/lifestyle (T1) and measurable health outcomes (T2). Existing evidence supports both but does not isolate which driver dominates purchase decisions. A low‑cost test can measure real commitment to each value proposition and reveal which mechanism is more likely to drive adoption.

**Teacher, uncertain:** It is unclear whether home users prioritize design/status or health‑benefit evidence when choosing premium fitness equipment. The market is also in flux, with many categories declining, making it hard to infer demand from trends alone. No direct data on willingness to pay for either proposition exists.

**Teacher, why next:** A test that offers two distinct prototypes—one positioned as a premium lifestyle experience and the other as a health‑focused solution—can capture real purchase intent (e.g., a refundable deposit). The conversion rates will directly test the core assumptions of T1 and T2, providing actionable evidence to decide whether to pursue a differentiated proposition and, if so, which angle to emphasize.

**Flags:** asks for a real commitment (deposit, pre-order, paid use)
