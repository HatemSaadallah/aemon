7. CANDIDATE DIRECTIONS TO CHALLENGE

Generate 4-6 meaningfully different candidates. Consider these starting points but do not force them all into one product:

- Experiment-selection agent: chooses which business experiment to run next under a budget, updates beliefs, and decides when to stop.
- Evidence-challenging agent: identifies a consequential assumption in a proposed strategy, proposes a competing explanation, and seeks evidence that could discriminate between them. It should also recognize when the original strategy is supported.
- Hypothesis-revision agent: detects when none of its original theories explains new observations and expands or revises the hypothesis space.
- Reliable analysis agent: selects and executes an appropriate statistical analysis, checks assumptions, and translates the result into a bounded business recommendation.
- Causal-evidence agent: retrieves evidence for specific causal links, distinguishes causal support from correlation, and identifies which uncertainty requires a new experiment.
- Stakeholder-belief agent: makes disagreements about assumptions explicit and chooses questions or tests that would help resolve them.

For each candidate specify the user, concrete decision, available tools, multi-step task, trainable behavior, data source, verifiable outcome, closest prior work, feasible contribution, failure modes, compute needs, and minimal demonstration.

9. EVALUATION AND REWARD DESIGN

Specify the claim BEFORE designing its evaluation. Distinguish tool-call correctness, task performance, scientific reasoning behavior, and effects on human decision-makers.

Use the same underlying model, tools, budgets, and held-out scenarios when comparing:
- Base model with a reasonable prompt.
- Base model with a carefully designed scientific-decision prompt/workflow.
- Fine-tuned model.
- A simple non-LLM policy and, if tractable, a stronger statistical/oracle reference.

A larger teacher model may be an extra reference, with its different compute cost made explicit. Hold out scenario families or causal structures as well as random seeds when feasible; do not claim generalization from renamed copies of training examples. Use validation for reward/prompt tuning and preserve a final test set.

Choose at least three meaningful metrics, potentially:
- Decision quality or regret: expected payoff lost relative to a defined optimal action in the simulated world. Define what the oracle knows; do not compare against clairvoyance about future random outcomes.
- Research cost and final payoff after those costs; avoid double-counting costs in regret and reward.
- Accuracy and calibration of explicit probabilities across many held-out predictions, using proper scoring rules such as Brier score or log loss where appropriate.
- Correct belief revision under evidence that supports or contradicts a hypothesis.
- Appropriate stopping or abstention.
- Valid tool calls, unnecessary calls, task completion, latency, and actual compute cost.

Expected information gain is not identical to business value. An experiment may reduce uncertainty about an irrelevant parameter while leaving the decision unchanged. Prefer value of information or decision-aware rewards when tractable, and explain any proxy.

For reinforcement learning, define a reward the environment can actually compute. Do not reward confidence, persuasive prose, always rejecting the user's idea, or merely following a checklist. Consider reward hacking through excessive testing, fake evidence, abstaining on every case, exploiting a simulator artifact, or calling a scoring/oracle tool.

Test matching cases where the user's initial hypothesis is right and wrong. Include noisy evidence, conflicting studies, redundant experiments, budget exhaustion, uninformative tests, and at least one shift from training conditions. An adversarial critique should improve truth-seeking, not just increase disagreement.

Use LLM judges only where necessary and document their weaknesses. Prefer executable checks for numerical facts and known simulated outcomes. Agreement among LLMs or a causal-looking graph is not proof of causal identification.

10. LIMITS THAT MUST REMAIN VISIBLE

- A synthetic environment demonstrates performance within defined assumptions. It does not validate real market demand or prove improved managerial outcomes.
- LLM-generated customer interviews are not independent empirical evidence of customer behavior.
- A confidence-calibrated agent does not automatically reduce human overconfidence; that would require a separate human study.
- The papers motivate hypotheses about an AI system. Human training interventions do not establish that the same intervention will work for an LLM.
- More agents, more tool calls, more experiments, and longer explanations are not inherently better.
- Public code availability does not establish reliable operation, a reusable license, or compatibility with our stack.
- A literature search can identify closest known work but cannot prove global novelty.
- We should report negative or mixed findings honestly, including when prompting or a simple policy beats fine-tuning.
